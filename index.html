Total Area Burned by State

Discussion: Relative Impact of Mega-Fires by State

While raw data often shows that the largest states like Western Australia and Queensland have the greatest total area burned, this can be misleading. Normalising the data by each state's total land area reveals a much more nuanced and insightful story about the relative impact of major bushfires across the country. This chart displays the cumulative percentage of each state's entire landmass that has been burned by mega-fires (>10,000 ha) since 1927.

The results are striking. We can see that the Australian Capital Territory (ACT) and Victoria, despite their small size, have experienced a disproportionately high impact from catastrophic fire events over the last century.

Key Findings

The ACT's Extreme Vulnerability: The most significant finding is the extreme vulnerability of the ACT. The data reveals that the 2003 Canberra bushfires burned an area equivalent to 111.5% of the territory's entire landmass. This astonishing figure highlights that a single fire event can consume an entire administrative region, posing a unique threat to the nation's capital.

Victoria's Repeated Catastrophes: Victoria shows the second-highest cumulative percentage burned. This is not due to one single event but a history of repeated, devastating fire seasons. Highlighting key years reveals the story: the 1939 "Black Friday" fires (14.7%), the 2006 fires (19.6%), and the 2009 "Black Saturday" fires (8.8%).

The Misleading Scale of Large States: In contrast, while Western Australia has a huge raw area burned, the normalised data proves that the relative impact on smaller, more densely populated states can be far more severe. This analysis confirms that simply measuring total hectares is not enough to understand the true impact of a fire season.

Discussion: The Rainfall Connection

There is a strong visible correlation between years with low rainfall and years with a high total area burned by mega-fires. The orange circles on the precipitation chart highlight years where rainfall dropped by more than 100mm compared to the year prior.

Finding: Our analysis shows that in these years of significant rainfall decline, there was an 80% chance of an increase in the total area burned by mega-fires. This provides strong evidence that drought conditions are a key driver of catastrophic fire seasons.
